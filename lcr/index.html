LCR Meter

< Back

The entry price for a capable LCR meter is around $100 NZD even on AliExpress, so I set out to make a cheaper LCR meter myself. I was also intrigued by the challenge of designing an LCR meter and have used this project to expand my knowledge of analogue electronics.

Initially I had no idea how to go about designing an LCR, but eventually I came across a Texas Instruments design guide (TIDA-060029) and used it as the basis for my own design. The figure below shows the LCR circuit stripped down to its most fundamental components and shows the equation that is used to calculate the device under test (DUT) impedance. If the phase relationship of the output voltages is known, then the phase of the DUT impedance is known. The LCR software is able to convert the complex impedance into an inductance or capacitance value + ESR. The MCU software uses the pk-pk values of the sampled waveforms for magnitudes, and calculates the phase difference using in-phase and quadrature components of the multiplied current and test voltage waveforms (v3 & v2). The IQ components are low-pass-filtered, then their arctan is taken to find the phase difference.


To allow measurement of a vast range of inductances and capacitances, the LCR has different range resistors. The first PCB revision used MOSFET bidirectional power switches to allow switching of range resistors. However, the MOSFETs had significant stray capacitance, introducing unwanted low pass filters into the signal path. To overcome this issue I switched to signal relays.

The first prototype PCB also had issues with noise being present in the LCR test waveform. I’m confident the noise is caused by oscillations in one of the opamps, likely caused by large stray complex impedances in the long jumper wires used for the temporary range resistors. Replacing the jumper wires with an on-PCB solution should fix this issue in the next revision.

Through multiple iterations I also had an issue with “noise” being present in the LCR voltage and current test waveforms. Initially I thought this noise came from the power supplies, but after testing I ruled out the possibility. Eventually, I realised I was having problems with opamp ringing due to stray capacitance in the traces. To solve this issue I switched from the high performance opamps I was using, to ones with a much lower gain bandwidth product, therefore more margin for stray component effects. Stray effects are only significant because of the increased length from routing multiple combinations of feedback resistors for the opamp (multiple range resistors).

The final design is able to measure a wide range of passive component values, but it’s accuracy hasn’t been tested against a calibrated lab instrument. The LCR features multiple test frequencies (0.1, 1, 10, 100 kHz), autoranging, and is able to notify the user when the DUT could not be autoranged for the desired test frequency. There is naturally some error in the measurements, since only one of the Kelvin probes is actually functioning as a Kelvin probe. Current must flow through the black probe, so its impedance is included in the measurement.
